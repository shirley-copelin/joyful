# Parent Discovery Interview Guide

**Interview length:** 45–60 minutes  
**Format:** Conversational, not survey. Follow energy over order.  
**Your role:** Curious, non-judgmental, unhurried. You are here to understand, not to sell.

---

## Before You Start

Say this (or something close to it):

> *"Thanks so much for making time. I'm doing research on how families find activities for their kids — I'm trying to understand what the experience is like before building anything. There are no right or wrong answers. I'm going to ask you to walk me through real experiences, not hypotheticals. If it's okay with you, I'd like to record just so I can focus on listening rather than taking notes — it won't be shared with anyone."*

---

## Section 1: Context (5 minutes)
*Goal: Understand this family's situation. Ground the conversation in their reality.*

- Tell me about your family — how old are your kids, and what are they into right now?
- What does a typical week look like for your family in terms of activities and schedule?
- How long have you lived in Lafayette / Lamorinda?

**Listen for:** How many kids, age range, activity intensity, how settled vs. newly arrived they are, whether they mention other parents or their kids' social lives.

---

## Section 2: The Discovery Experience (15 minutes)
*Goal: Understand how they find activities today — the process, tools, people, and emotions involved.*

### Opening
- Walk me through the last time you found a new activity for one of your kids. Take me back to the beginning — what was going on that made you start looking?

**Listen for:** The trigger (child asked, season change, friend mentioned something, felt their kid needed something new). Don't rush past this — the trigger is gold.

### Deepening
- Where did you go first?
- Who did you talk to?
- How did you end up deciding on the thing you chose?
- Were there things you considered and ruled out? What made you pass on them?

### The trust question
- When you were looking, what made you trust one recommendation over another?
- Has a recommendation ever not panned out? What happened?
- If a stranger on Yelp and a parent you know both said the same thing was great, would you weigh them the same?

**Probe if yes:** *Tell me about that.* **Probe if no:** *What's the difference for you?*

### The effort question
- How long did that whole process take — from when you started looking to when your kid was actually signed up?
- Does that feel like the right amount of time to spend on it, or does it feel like more than it should be?
- Is this a one-time thing or does this happen frequently for your family?

**Listen for:** Frequency of discovery cycles, time investment, any resignation or frustration in how they describe it.

---

## Section 3: The Coordination Experience (10 minutes)
*Goal: Understand what happens after discovery — scheduling, logistics, social coordination.*

- Once you decide on an activity, what does it take to actually make it happen?
- What tools do you use to manage your family's schedule — calendar, group chats, anything else?
- Tell me about a time when coordinating an activity got complicated. What happened?

### The social layer
- Does it matter to you whether your kids' friends are doing the same activities?
- Have you ever made an activity decision partly because another family you know was doing it? Tell me about that.
- Have you ever coordinated carpools for an activity? What did that look like?

**Listen for:** The weight of the social/community dimension. Is coordination just logistics, or is there a relationship layer that matters?

---

## Section 4: Pain and Workarounds (10 minutes)
*Goal: Find the real friction — the moments that are broken enough that parents have invented workarounds.*

- What's the most frustrating part of the whole process — finding activities, coordinating, managing the schedule?
- What do you do today that you wish you didn't have to do?
- Is there a group chat or spreadsheet or system you've set up just to manage this stuff? Tell me about it.
- What falls through the cracks most often?

### The emotional question (ask carefully, it opens things up)
- How do you feel at the end of a week where activities went smoothly vs. a week where things were chaotic?
- Do you ever feel like the logistics are getting in the way of actually enjoying time with your kids?

**Listen for:** Language of guilt, anxiety, overwhelm, or conversely — delight and community. These emotional signals tell you whether this is a vitamin or a painkiller.

---

## Section 5: Identity and Values (5 minutes)
*Goal: Understand what kind of parent they want to be — the deeper job under the surface job.*

- When you think about the activities your kids are in, what are you hoping they get out of them?
- Is there a difference between what you want for them and what they want for themselves? How do you navigate that?
- If you could describe the ideal version of how this part of your family life works — the activities, the schedule, the community — what would it look like?

**Listen for:** The aspirational identity. "I want my kids to have a well-rounded childhood." "I want them to find their thing." "I want us to be connected to community." These phrases become your marketing.

---

## Section 6: Concept Reaction (5 minutes, optional — only if time allows)
*Goal: Get a raw, unconstructed reaction to the concept before you've explained it fully.*

Say this, not more:

> *"I'll share what I'm working on now that I've heard your experience. Imagine an app that knew your kids — their ages, interests, what they've tried — and showed you activity recommendations filtered through what parents you actually know have tried and trusted. Not strangers. People you recognize. And it could help coordinate who's going to what with which families. Does that resonate with anything you've described?"*

Then stop talking. Let them react.

**Listen for:**
- First word or phrase they use
- Whether they say "I would use that" or "interesting" (very different signals)
- What they immediately ask about or push back on
- Whether they bring up a specific pain point from earlier in the conversation

**Follow-up only if they're engaged:**
- What would make you actually try something like that?
- What would make you trust it with your family's information?
- What would make you pay for it?

---

## Closing

> *"This has been incredibly helpful. Last question: is there anything about this topic — finding activities, managing family logistics, community with other parents — that you wish I'd asked about? Anything that came to mind during our conversation that feels important?"*

Then: Thank them, confirm the gift card / coffee, ask if you can follow up with one or two questions later if something comes up.

---

## What to Listen For Across All Interviews

### High-signal moments
- They lean forward or their voice changes
- They say "I hate that" or "that drives me crazy"
- They describe a workaround they invented
- They laugh ruefully
- They say "every parent I know deals with this"
- They use a specific phrase you've heard before (pattern forming)

### Red flags (things that mean less than they seem)
- "I would definitely use that" (they're being polite; watch behavior not stated intent)
- Generic complaints without specific stories
- Hypothetical answers ("I would probably..." vs. "I did...")
- Agreement without elaboration

### The golden question you can always fall back on
**"Tell me more about that."** It works at any point in any interview. When in doubt, say it.
